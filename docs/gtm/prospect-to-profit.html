00 The whole thing

Lead to revenue, in one picture

Seven stages across the top, the systems that do the work down the side. Two vertical lines matter more than anything else on the page: the qualification gate, left of which nothing touches the CRM, and signature, which is the only moment money starts.

The shaded band is the write boundary. Prospector, the record and Command Center sit inside it. ContentPro and invoicing sit outside and receive exports, which is what stops four systems from each holding their own version of the truth.

Part One

Find

Turning a stranger into someone who wants a conversation. This is the part that used to be luck.

01 The shift

The reports move in front of the conversation

Today a lead arrives, gets a meeting link, and the war report and competitor report are prepared for a call that is already booked. The research supports a conversation that inbound already won.

Inverted, the same two reports become the reason the conversation happens. The work is the outreach. A roofer who receives a genuine audit of their site and three named competitors has been given something before being asked for anything.

02 Step one, and it is not built yet

Today the lead still arrives. Tomorrow we go and find it

Everything in the previous section describes the motion we are building, not the one running now. Right now a lead arrives by email or contact form, gets a meeting link, and the reports are prepared for a conversation that already happened. The half that finds the prospect in the first place is a separate script that has never been connected to anything.

That script exists and it works. It queries Google Maps for a trade across a list of markets, pulls place details, and scores every result by digital weakness:

ScoreWhat it meansWhat to do
HotNo website, or almost no reviewsCall this week
WarmHas a site, weak presenceRun the audit first
ColdEstablished digital presenceSkip for now

It outputs a CSV, an interactive dashboard and optionally a Google Sheet. Roughly five to eight dollars of Places API spend gets sixty results across five markets, which is the cheapest lead sourcing in the business by a wide margin.

What is missing is not the research, it is the connection. The scored prospect lands in a spreadsheet and stops. Nothing mints a client record, nothing chains the audit, nothing tracks whether the email was opened. Folding this into Prospector as a first engine that writes to the record is step one, and it is what turns a list into a pipeline.

Two things to get right when it moves in. The market list and the trade keyword belong in the vertical pack, not hardcoded in the script. And the scoring thresholds should be visible and tunable, because what counts as a weak presence is different for a roofer than for an electrician.

03 The gate

Records are born local and promoted into the CRM

Running war reports on forty roofers should not put forty companies into the CRM. Prospector is a research tool, and research is cheap; a CRM full of companies nobody ever spoke to is worse than useless because it destroys the meaning of the pipeline count.

So a client record starts as crmSource: "local" and lives entirely inside Prospector. It crosses into the CRM at one point only.

Yes, pushed in rather than pulled from. A prospect who already exists in the CRM is picked from search and pulled down instead, but that is the smaller path. In an outbound motion most records are born in Prospector and promoted, which is why the pipeline count stays honest.

04 What fires the gate

Open events, not a judgment call

Sourcing from Google Places gives more than a name and a URL. An unclaimed profile, thin photos, no Posts and a review count under the market median say a prospect has an addressable problem and that nobody is already selling them. Score the list on those signals rather than only personalizing from them, so the send queue is ranked by opportunity instead of alphabet.

Then reuse what already exists. Prospector mints publish tokens and serves hosted pages at /p/:token for proposals, with views logged. Point that at the war and competitor reports and the send becomes a short personal note plus a link, which beats a PDF attachment on deliverability and produces the signal below.

Framing decides the reply more than specificity does. "Your Google profile is unclaimed and you have 12 reviews" is the most-used cold SEO opener in existence and gets categorized by line two. "Fischer has been on Queen Anne forty years with an unclaimed profile and two photos, Bob Oates posted yesterday and has 767 reviews, here is the gap that leaves" is the same data handed over as market intelligence. The test: if the opening line survives a find-and-replace of the company name, it is a merge field and it will read as one.

06 The full pathway

Four systems, one record, six stages

The engines are stages in one lifecycle rather than separate tools. Everything left of signature is a sales artifact and lives locally; everything right of it is a delivery obligation. The boundaries between apps matter because they decide who is allowed to write.

The reports are a menu, not a sequence. Local SEO, organic, paid and silo plans usually run after close, but nothing stops them running earlier and one of them is often what closes the deal. Gate them with a warning, never a block: a salesperson asking for a local SEO report on an unreplied prospect may have a reason the system cannot see. The one real dependency is that the silo plan inherits positioning, so building it before the strategy settles means building it twice.

07 One contract, four apps

The record outgrew the repo it was written in

The client record is not a Prospector feature. It is a contract that Command Center, Prospector, invoicing and ContentPro all implement against. It was written inside Prospector’s docs folder because that is where the work happened, and that was wrong in a specific way: a change to the contract appeared in one app’s history and nowhere else, and the app that owns the record had no record of the contract it implements.

The contract repo holds the record and its JSON schema, the boundary rules, both export contracts, and a versioning policy that says what may change additively and what has to be announced to every consumer first.

The rule that stops it fragmenting again: a consumer repo documents its implementation, never the contract. Tables, migrations, endpoints, storage decisions, all of that is local. A restatement of what the record holds or who may write it is a copy, and copies drift. That is the whole reason for the move.

Two corollaries came out of the first consumer reading it, and both are the keying rule applied somewhere new. A billing-system id is not a key. QuickBooks and Stripe customer ids are references the record holds. Neither is a proposal id. Billing artifacts key to the client, not to one sales document, or a second proposal orphans the first one’s invoices.

Part Three

Sell

What we send, what it costs to produce, and what it is allowed to promise.

08 Two documents, one state

The person who signs is not the person the strategy was written for

The strategy document is written to survive scrutiny, which is the right way to write it. The owner of a one-truck plumbing business is not the person doing the scrutinizing. On the first engagement the owner read all sixty-three pages and asked, politely, whether there was a shorter one. There was not. Now there is, and it ships as part of the package rather than as a favor.

Most of the brief is not written at all. The service area tiers, the cost tables, the blocking-items list and the next-steps tables already exist as structured data, so they reformat straight through with no model call. Five sections get a constrained rewrite, and those receive the rendered source text as their only input under one hard rule: introduce no fact that is not in it.

Nine QA rules police the result. Two carry the weight. B1 extracts every numeric token from both documents and fails on anything in the brief that is absent from the full one, which is what turns one-state-two-renders from an intention into something the gate checks. B5 requires every blocking item in the full document to survive into the brief: the ask may be reworded, it may not be made smaller. Softening the ask is what happens naturally when a document is rewritten to be friendlier, and a softened ask is how a launch slips three weeks.

Keep the cautions. A summary that drops every warning becomes a sales document, and the owner meets the warning later as a surprise. Four survive by rule: leads do not arrive in month one, set the ad budget from capacity rather than ambition, the second technician breaks the service promises, and the promises need software behind them before they are published.

09 Scope

Four projects in five are not brand projects

Brand and messaging work applies to about one engagement in five. For the rest, forty pages of identity analysis is effort nobody bought and it makes the document read as padded. Both toggles default off, using the same section-flag mechanism as the trade gates.

Sections renumber rather than leaving gaps. A document jumping from 10 to 13 tells the client something was removed, and a cross-reference pointing at a dropped section is a blocking error whose fix is rewriting the sentence, never re-enabling the section.

10 The gates by trade

One pipeline, branching on the vertical pack

A roofer and a landscaper need genuinely different documents, not the same document with the service list swapped. The vertical pack carries a pipeline block that decides which analyses run and which sections exist, so engine code never branches on trade.

Part Four

Deliver

The part clients actually pay for, and the handover that usually loses a fortnight.

11 Onboarding, and the two exports

The moment the record stops being a sales artifact and becomes a project

A signature does not finish anything. It starts the part of the business that actually costs money to run, and the handover between the two is where agencies routinely lose a fortnight retyping what they already knew.

Onboard lives in Command Center because it is the last place the record is written before delivery begins. It does three things: collects access, sets the project up, and resolves the open brackets the strategy left behind. Every bracket in a delivered document is a question only the client can answer, and the list of them is already in section 18 of their strategy. Onboarding is where that list gets closed, not where it gets rediscovered.

Then two exports leave the write boundary, and neither one is a copy of the record. Each is a purpose-built snapshot.

ContentPro receives the client profile, the sitemap, the content plan and the documents themselves as a knowledge base. That last part is the difference between a content tool producing plausible copy and one writing from a researched position. Invoicing receives the commercial terms and the project shape, and nothing else. It does not need to know the competitive landscape to raise an invoice.

Both exports are validated before they are written, not after they are read. The ContentPro payload in particular has to contain zero efelle content and zero reference to any other client, which is a rule its own isolation model depends on and which is far cheaper to enforce at the boundary.

12 How a site actually gets built

Five hands, in order, with account management across all of them

The strategy and the proposal are the cheap part. This is the part that consumes the studio, and it is the reason intake rate and delivery capacity are the same conversation.

Account management runs across the whole chain rather than sitting at the end of it. That is the role that owns the client’s experience of a process which otherwise hands them between four sets of people, and it is the one function in this document that does not automate.

The style tile is the highest-leverage checkpoint in delivery. Agreement on color, type and tone before layout starts is what keeps a semi-custom build semi-custom. Every project that goes over does so because that agreement was assumed rather than signed.

The upstream half of this document exists to make this chain start from a researched position. The style tile inherits a palette the strategy already argued for; the pages inherit a sitemap the strategy already justified; the content inherits a position rather than a brief. Nothing here starts blank, which is where the hours normally go.

13 Launch is a marketing event

The one moment a client is proud of the work, and it is usually wasted

A site goes live and nothing happens. The team moves to the next build, the client tells a few people, and the single best piece of proof efelle produced that month never leaves the invoice.

The social image builder exists to close that. It takes a launched site and produces branded graphics in four formats, launch, deck slide, stat and update, plus story carousels, screenshotting the live site with Playwright and rendering video and animated versions through ffmpeg. It is a local Flask app today, with a stock imagery integration and a handful of finishing items outstanding: a start script, an image upload interface, faster preview, and API verification.

Where it belongs in this pipeline: launch, triggered by account management, drawing the client name, palette and site URL straight from the record. The record already holds every input the builder needs, which means this is a small integration rather than a new tool.

What it produces feeds two audiences at once. The client gets something to post, which is the cheapest goodwill available at exactly the moment goodwill is highest. And efelle gets a portfolio entry, a proof point for the next proposal, and a piece of the case-study library that the strategy engine cites.

14 The recurring program

The part of the business that is worth building

A website is a project. Projects end. The Revenue Growth Service is the part that does not, and it is the reason the whole motion at the front of this document is worth automating: a cheaper way to find one client is only interesting if that client is worth something for years.

The program is scoped and priced per client rather than sold from a rate card, because a one-truck plumber and a three-branch manufacturer need genuinely different work. What does not vary is the shape: local search, organic, paid, reviews, reporting, and a monthly conversation about what the numbers did.

Hosting and support sits alongside it at $145 a month today, covering CMS updates and upgrades, server maintenance and support. It is not a profit center on its own. It is what keeps the site the program is optimizing from decaying underneath it. The model later in this document reprices it to $65 and the program to $2,250, on the argument that the money belongs on the line that does the growing and hosting should be priced to be an easy yes. Those are a proposal, not today’s list.

Recurring charges start at launch, not at signature. That single rule is written into the invoicing contract as a field rather than left to memory, because it is the most common billing argument in this business and it is entirely avoidable.

Part Five

Profit

Where the money is, where it is not, and the two numbers we cannot yet supply.

15 What a client is worth

The build pays for the work. The program pays for the business

Everything before this section is cost. This is where it turns over.

The standard ladder is a work order for updates to an existing site, a template build at $7,500, a semi-custom build at $12,500, and a fully custom build quoted. Semi-custom is the default and the one most home-services engagements land on. Where a project also buys strategy and launch content, as Nic’s Plumbing did, the one-time total is larger and the shape is identical: half to start, the rest over twenty-four interest-free months.

Set against that, the recurring side is smaller every month and larger every year. It is also the only part that survives a project ending.

Ad spend never touches efelle. The client pays the channel directly, on their own card, on their own account. It is not revenue, it is not pass-through, and it does not appear anywhere in efelle’s books. Agencies that route it and let it sit in the top line flatter themselves and then make capacity decisions from a number that was never theirs.

Two numbers this document cannot yet supply, and they are the ones that decide how hard to push the engine: what it costs to acquire a client through this motion, and how long a program client stays. Neither can be estimated honestly today. Both fall straight out of the instrumentation in the measurement section, which is the argument for building it early rather than when someone asks.

16 Getting paid

Three systems, one field coming back

Billing is where the boundary rules earn their keep, because it is the place where a second source of truth is most tempting and most damaging.

The invoicing app reads the offer export and builds the schedule: deposit, installments, recurring charges with their start rule. It matches or creates the QuickBooks customer. It raises the invoices. Then the A/R bot chases what has not been paid, which is a job nobody wants and which a small team will otherwise do late or not at all. Payments are moving to Stripe ACH, which lowers the cost of collecting the recurring side in particular.

Invoicing writes exactly one field back to the client record: payment status. Whether a client is current is a fact the rest of the business needs. Nothing else invoicing knows is. A QuickBooks customer id is a reference the record holds, never a key, and neither is a proposal id. Billing artifacts key to the client, or a second proposal orphans the first one’s invoices.

The reason to be strict about this is not tidiness. A billing system that is allowed to write freely becomes the de facto client list within a quarter, because it is the one system that is always up to date about who is paying. At that point there are two client lists and they disagree.

17 Compounding

The tenth client in a market is nearly free

Every engagement produces research that is not client-specific. The census pull for Seattle ZIPs, the competitor set for plumbing in Queen Anne, the King County permit file: none of it belongs to one client, it belongs to the market. Treated as per-client work, the second plumber in Ballard costs the same to research as the first.

Cache it under data/markets/{metro}/ and only the client-specific parts stay fresh.

Two rules keep it safe. Everything cached carries a vintage and a source, because a shared fact with no provenance becomes a liability the moment a client questions it. And client-specific interpretation never enters the cache: the census table is shared, but "98117 is the strongest fit for this brand" belongs to one client and stays in their record.

19 What an investor would need to see

Almost none of the recurring book is contracted, and it is the cheap part that is

The operating targets that a growth investor or a strategic buyer will test are recurring growth of 25 to 40 percent a year and net revenue retention of 110 to 120 percent. Growth is not the problem here. Retention is, and the reason sits inside the recurring line. What all of it is finally worth is the section called Selling it, near the end, on an EBITDA basis; this one is about the two operating numbers that decide which bracket that lands in.

That is the argument for the next part.

Should the program carry a term, and how long

The program is sold on a three month initial term and then runs month to month. That is the arrangement to examine, because three months is the one length the research below argues against. Published benchmarks put annual churn for full-service digital agencies at about 25%, for standalone SEO at 38% and for PPC at 49%. Mid-market B2B software sits at 5 to 10%. The gap is not about quality, it is structural: software embeds in workflows and is painful to leave, while agency services can be paused or taken in-house on thirty days notice. efelle has more lock-in than most, because the client’s site runs on efelle’s own platform, but 25% is the honest planning number for a program sold month to month. The model defaults to 10% because it also assumes the program carries the 24-month term, and the term is what earns the lower number. Take the term away and 25% is what to plan on.

The research names the mechanism behind SEO’s churn precisely, and it is the argument for a term: clients expect rankings in 30 to 60 days when the work takes three to six months to show return. A month-to-month program on a service with that lag is structurally selling people the option to leave one month before it starts working.

Reward the term, but not with a lower monthly fee. On lifetime profit you could discount up to 43% and still come out ahead, because a longer life is worth that much. On exit ARR the break-even is only 12%, and a 15% discount leaves you worse off than staying month to month. The discount hits every client every month immediately; the retention benefit accrues slowly. If the goal is a sale, those two objectives point in opposite directions and the sale objective is the binding one.

Pay for the commitment with one-time value instead: a build credit, waived setup, an extra service for the first quarter, a higher ad-management allowance. Those cost once. The monthly fee is the number that gets multiplied.

Part Six

The machine

What exists today, what breaks, and what to build next.

20 The systems, and the state of each

What exists, what is being built, and what is still a document

The honest reading of this board is that the front of the motion is further along than the middle. The tools that find and warm a prospect are shipped. The record that is supposed to carry that prospect through to a paying client is still being built, and until it is, every handover between these systems is a human retyping something.

The CRM row is the one open decision. HubSpot is in place and about eighty percent of the integration work is already written. Seedly is bought and would remove a substantial monthly bill. The adapter exists so that this can be decided on cost and fit rather than on sunk integration work, which is the only sensible basis for it.

21 The states that are not a win

Most prospects do not convert, and that is the pipeline

Everything so far describes forward motion. In an outbound engine the majority of records stop somewhere, and the ones that stop this quarter are next year's list. What makes them worth keeping is that the research is already done and can be refreshed rather than rebuilt.

Suppression is not deletion. A passed record keeps its research so a re-approach in two years costs a refresh, and so nobody emails them again by accident next quarter. A churned client is the warmest cold prospect in the database: they have read the reports, they know the work, and something changed.

22 When it ends

Churn is a normal event, so it needs a path rather than an improvisation

The program runs on a three month initial term and then month to month with thirty days notice, and the investor section argues for lengthening that term. Either way clients leave, some of them for reasons that have nothing to do with the work, and a term changes when that happens rather than whether it does.

Five questions are worth answering once, in advance, rather than under pressure on the day. Which access transfers and which was ours: the Google profile, analytics, the ad accounts. Whether hosting hands over or stops. Who owns what ContentPro produced. What happens to the ad account history, which has real value to whoever holds it. And what becomes of the record.

Archive the record, never delete it. A churned client is the warmest cold prospect the business will ever have. They have the reports, they know the work, and something changed. Keep the record intact and requeue it on a longer cycle.

25 Potential FusionCMS future state

The build list that decides whether fulfillment is labor or software

The platform already does more than a content management system: it reads the company, trade, market, competitors and season, builds a content plan from that, drips it to the site, and reports on what moved. The list below is what it could do next. It is a product roadmap, not a repackaging exercise, and the reason to care about the order is in the next section: the difference between an agency multiple and a tech-enabled one is decided by how much human labor sits between the client paying and the work happening.

The test for every item on this list

Does a person touch it on the way to the client every month? Not whether it could in principle be automated. Whether, in the month it is delivered, someone at efelle has to do something for it to happen. Everything that clears that test lifts the margin and moves efelle toward the higher bracket. Everything that does not is a service, and some of it should stay one.

The approval workflow is the unlock

Content generation is the item that decides how much of the program is software, and it turns entirely on whether anything publishes without a person in the loop. The workable pattern is not “approve everything” or “approve nothing”:

  1. Classify at generation. Routine work, service pages, seasonal posts, job write-ups and photo captions, goes in one bucket. Anything touching licensing, code compliance, safety, guarantees, pricing or a competitor claim goes in the other.
  2. Routine work publishes on a timer. Notify the client, give a fixed window of five business days to object, publish if nobody does. The client keeps control and almost never uses it, which is the point.
  3. The second bucket always gets a human. A plumbing client is regulated, and a confidently wrong sentence about permits or backflow certification is a real liability, not a typo.

The five day window is what converts a review queue into a publishing system. Without it, every piece waits on a client who is on a roof, and the labor to chase them is the labor the software was supposed to remove.

Build the moat around the data and the habit, not around the writing. Content generation is the most exposed item here: in three years a general purpose model will write a service page as well as anything purpose-built, and a buyer doing diligence in 2029 will ask exactly that. What is not replicable is the market data assembled per metro and per trade over years, the census pulls, competitor sets, regulatory files and search history, and the phone-to-profile habit that puts efelle inside a plumber’s working day. Vertical depth is the defensible part, and home services is about as vertical as it gets.

What has to be measured for any of this to count

  1. Cost per client per month to run the platform. API calls, hosting, storage, the real number. Without it the margin claim in the next section is an assertion.
  2. Hours per client per month on the service side, tracked from now, not reconstructed later. This is the number that moves as the list above gets built, and it is the evidence that fulfillment is not labor-bound.
  3. A support path that is not the account manager. If platform questions land on the person who runs the campaigns, the labor is still there and the margin improvement is fiction.
  4. Publish rate without human touch. The share of content that went live on the timer. One number, tracked monthly, that says how far along this actually is.

This is a roadmap of possibilities, not a commitment or a schedule. Sequencing should follow what removes the most recurring labor per engineering week, which is why reporting and profile sync sit at the top and citation building is a purchase rather than a project.

26 Selling it, and what it is worth

A tech-enabled service, priced on EBITDA, and the margin is the whole argument

The plan is a sale roughly three years from the point efelle becomes a home-services recurring-revenue business. That makes valuation an operating question now rather than a finance question later, because everything that moves the number takes years to put in place.

One basis, and it is not a revenue multiple

efelle is a tech-enabled service and will be priced on EBITDA, not on ARR. The rule buyers apply is explicit: where software delivery depends on manual agency labor, the recurring revenue is stripped of any software multiple and priced as services, however faithfully it recurs. That is not a reason to plan around a lower number. The tech-enabled bracket, eight to twelve times EBITDA on a 30% margin, gets to a higher answer than a revenue multiple on an agency margin does, and it gets there without unbundling the offer into standalone software licenses that would have to be priced, supported and sold on their own.

The whole question is therefore which of two brackets efelle lands in, and the thing that decides it is whether fulfillment still runs on people. That is what the FusionCMS section before this one is a plan for.

Do not read that as churn being harmless. A 24-month term means the first cohorts only reach their renewal in month 25, so a 36-month window sees barely a year of churn on the earliest clients and none at all on the rest. The term protects the numbers a buyer looks back at, which is convenient, and a competent buyer knows it. They will underwrite forward, ask what happens in years four and five, and discount the answer unless there is renewal evidence to point at. That is why the renewal record matters more than the churn assumption.

Two of the four are already strong, and one of those is unusually so. Customer concentration is the silent deal-killer in agency transactions, and a book of 344 home-services clients at roughly $2,300 a month has effectively none. That is a structural advantage of selling to small trades rather than to a handful of large accounts, and it is easy to overlook while wishing for bigger clients.

Labor per client is the one that is not just unproven but unmeasured, and it is the exact number a buyer will ask for first. Hours per client per month, tracked from now, falling as the platform takes work over, is the evidence for the 30% margin and the tech-enabled bracket. Reconstructed later from memory it is worth nothing.

What to have in place by the sale

  1. Three years of clean, separated financials showing recurring and project revenue apart, with the margin on each. Start now; it cannot be reconstructed.
  2. A renewal rate you can evidence, which means a term, an anniversary, and a recorded decision at each one. Month to month produces no renewal data at all, which is a second and quieter cost of selling it that way.
  3. Time tracking per client per month, from now, as the proof that delivery is not labor-bound.
  4. Client relationships held by named people who are not the founder, recorded in the client record rather than in anyone’s head.
  5. The engine demonstrably running without you, with the metrics from the measurement section to prove it.
  6. The loss-reason data. Fifty recorded losses with reasons is a defensible answer to the first hard question in diligence, which is always why clients leave.

Margins of 20% and 30% are assumptions supplied for the comparison, not measurements of efelle. Multiple ranges are benchmarks and they move with the market, the buyer and the deal structure; buyers also discount project revenue relative to recurring, which this table does not do. Nothing here is a valuation, a solicitation or investment advice, and I am not a valuation advisor. Before planning against any of it, get efelle’s actual EBITDA margin measured and have a broker or an M&A advisor with agency transaction experience mark the range.

27 What we do not know yet

The questions that should be answered by evidence rather than by argument

  1. What does a client acquired through this motion actually cost? Until the engine is instrumented, every claim about its efficiency is a guess.
  2. How long does a program client stay? This is the multiplier on everything else, and the business has the history to answer it before the engine produces a single new client.
  3. HubSpot or Seedly. Decide on cost and fit. Eighty percent of an integration being finished is not a reason to keep paying for the other system.
  4. Does Prospector become a module inside Command Center, or does Command Center wrap it? The record lives at the Command Center level either way.
  5. Who owns refreshing the market cache, and how often? It is the highest-leverage asset described here and the easiest to let rot.
  6. What is the retention play for a churned client, and does it run through this system or somewhere else?
  7. Multi-location clients break the service area model. Out of scope today. Worth naming as a decision rather than discovering as a surprise mid-build.
